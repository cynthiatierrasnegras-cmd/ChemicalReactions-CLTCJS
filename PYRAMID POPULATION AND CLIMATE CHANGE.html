Session 3·The demographic transition model

The 4 stages of demographic transition

Every country follows a path from high birth + death rates to low birth + death rates, driven by development, urbanization, and education.

01

Pre-industrial

No modern medicine. Frequent famine and disease. Many births but also many deaths. Population barely grows.

Birth: HIGHDeath: HIGH

Example: Most of human history before 1800

02

Early development

Medicine improves. Death rate falls fast. Birth rate still high. Population EXPLODES — not more births, but fewer deaths.

Birth: HIGHDeath: FALLING

Example: Mexico 1950s–1970s

03

Late development

Urbanization and education cause birth rate to fall. Growth slows. Pyramid base narrows. Demographic bonus window opens.

Birth: FALLINGDeath: LOW

Example: Brazil, Indonesia, Mexico today

04

Post-industrial

Both rates very low. High cost of living, career priorities, late marriages. Pyramid becomes rectangular or inverted.

Birth: LOWDeath: LOW

Example: Japan, Germany, South Korea

→ Mexico is currently transitioning between Stage 3 and Stage 4
The demographic transition curve — as development increases, death rate falls first, then birth rate follows. The gap between the two curves = population growth.

Session 3·The bridge to climate change

What happens when climate change enters the picture?

If millions are forced to leave their homes — what happens to the pyramid of the place they left? And the place they arrived?

OUT
Origin — place people leave
  • Young adults leave first — mobile and seeking opportunity
  • Working-age bars (20–45) shrink dramatically
  • Elderly and children stay — those least able to cope
  • Economy collapses — fewer workers, less tax revenue
  • Schools close, hospitals understaffed, infrastructure fails
IN
Destination — place people arrive
  • Sudden increase in young working-age adults
  • Housing demand spikes — rents rise, informal settlements grow
  • Pressure on schools, hospitals, water, and transport
  • Can be an opportunity IF managed well with planning
This is not hypothetical. It is already happening in Mexico, Bangladesh, the Sahel, and the Pacific islands right now.
How climate migration reshapes a population pyramid — the working-age bars (20–45) disappear from communities that experience climate displacement.

Session 3·When a country's pyramid disappears entirely

Tuvalu & Kiribati: the ultimate case

Pacific island nations whose entire populations may be forced to relocate permanently — the most extreme demographic consequence of climate change ever recorded.

Tuvalu — low-lying Pacific atoll nation. The red dashed line marks 2 meters above sea level. Most of the country is below this line. Source: Illustrated based on NASA sea level data.
Discussion question
If Tuvalu's 11,000 people move to New Zealand, what happens to New Zealand's pyramid? Does a nation still exist if its land is underwater?
ELV

Less than 2 meters above sea level

Average elevation across all of Tuvalu. Highest point: only 4.6 m. The entire country is essentially flat — and surrounded by a rising ocean.

RISE

Sea level rising 3.9 mm per year

Nearly twice the global average in Tuvalu's waters. At this rate, 95% of the capital Funafuti could be regularly flooded by 2100 (NASA projection).

2023

Historic agreement with New Zealand

Tuvalu signed a formal agreement allowing all 11,000 citizens to migrate and receive New Zealand residency — the first time in history a nation prepared to relocate its entire population due to climate change.

2025

The UN ruled: a nation can survive without land

The International Court of Justice ruled in 2025 that Tuvalu remains a nation-state with rights over its ocean resources and a seat at the UN — even if its islands disappear underwater.

120K

Kiribati: 120,000 people at equal risk

The larger neighbor faces the same existential threat. The Kiribati government has already purchased land in Fiji as a potential future home for its population.

Session 4·The most vulnerable groups

Who is most vulnerable to climate change?

These groups appear directly on the population pyramid — which means the pyramid itself is a vulnerability map.

Elderly 65+Top of the pyramid ↑

The elderly cannot escape heat

The body's ability to regulate temperature declines with age. The elderly are far more likely to die during heatwaves, and more likely to have chronic illness worsened by extreme weather. They are often less mobile — they cannot flee easily.

Real data: During Europe's 2003 heatwave, ~70,000 people died. The majority were elderly. France alone lost nearly 15,000 people in two weeks.
Children 0–5Base of the pyramid ↓

Children depend entirely on adults

Young children cannot regulate temperature or make survival decisions. Contaminated water after floods causes fatal disease. Food insecurity causes malnutrition — permanent developmental damage in the first 1,000 days of life.

UNICEF data: Children in the most climate-vulnerable countries face up to 7× more heatwaves and 3× more river floods than children born in 1960.
Rural poorAll age groups

Rural communities lose everything at once

A drought doesn't just make life uncomfortable — it destroys the harvest, eliminates income, and removes the food supply simultaneously. Fewer resources to adapt or relocate. Government disaster response reaches cities first, rural areas last or never.

Mexico context: States like Oaxaca, Chiapas, and Guerrero have large indigenous and rural populations with very limited adaptive capacity.
Informal workersWorking age (20–50)

Outdoor workers cannot shelter from heat

Construction workers, street vendors, agricultural laborers, and delivery workers cannot work from home or in air-conditioned offices. Extreme heat directly cuts their ability to work and their income. No formal protections, no sick leave, no savings.

Mexico context: ~56% of Mexico's workforce is in the informal economy — one of the highest rates in Latin America.

Session 4·Mexico's three climate frontlines

Mexico's three climate frontlines

Mexico is essentially three countries in terms of climate risk — and each threat affects a different part of the population pyramid.

DROUGHT

Northern Mexico

Climate change is intensifying droughts. In 2021, nearly 85% of Mexico faced drought — the worst in 30 years. The Rio Bravo reached critically low levels. Agriculture collapsed in key farming states.

Pyramid effect: Empties working-age bars (25–45) from rural towns. Young adults migrate to cities or the US. Tarahumara and other indigenous communities face severe food insecurity.
FLOODING

Coastal Mexico

Warmer oceans intensify hurricanes. In 2020, two hurricanes hit Tabasco in the same week — over 1 million people affected, 70% of the state underwater. Coastal communities face repeated, permanent displacement.

Pyramid effect: Repeated flooding causes permanent out-migration. Young mobile adults leave; elderly stay. Communities progressively age and lose economic capacity.
HEAT

Mexican cities

Urban "heat island effect" makes cities significantly hotter than surrounding areas. Mexicali and Hermosillo regularly exceed 45°C in summer. Mexico City set heat records despite its altitude. Number of extreme heat days increasing every decade.

Pyramid effect: Increases mortality in 65+ bars. Reduces productivity of outdoor workers (20–50 bars). Deepens inequality between rich and poor neighborhoods.

Session 4·Synthesis — the pyramid as a climate tool

The pyramid as a climate tool

A pyramid doesn't just show where people ARE — it shows who is AT RISK and what a place will NEED.

01

Climate change hits the most vulnerable age groups hardest

The elderly at the top and children at the base face the greatest physical risks. They are also the bars that shrink or grow in response to climate mortality events.

02

Climate migration is a demographic event

When people move because of climate, they reshape the pyramid of both the place they leave and the place they arrive. Every migration writes itself into the data.

03

Knowing who lives where is the first step in climate planning

Before any government can build shelters, design water programs, or expand cities, it needs demographic data. The pyramid is not academic — it is a planning tool.
